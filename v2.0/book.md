### Chapter 2 — The Cargo of Last Light

The train left the capital at 19:04, seven minutes behind schedule, and already the fire was visible from the yard.

Tomas watched it from the cab window—a column of orange climbing the sky above the Ministry of Information, the old colonial headquarters with its stone lions and shattered glass. The mob had gotten in. He'd heard the first shots as he was coupling the water tender, and now the station itself was a wound of screaming and running. Men in dusty uniforms pushed through the crowd with rifles held across their chests like holy objects.

They were all leaving. That was the thing. The British had been leaving for months, in their quiet, bureaucratic way—shipping out files and furniture and families, pretending it was all very orderly. But this was not orderly. This was a city eating itself, and the last train out was his to drive.

Tomas wiped his hand across his mouth. Coal dust and sweat. He'd been in the yard since dawn, coaxing the old Class 12 locomotive back to something resembling life. Number 407. She'd been slated for scrap before the troubles, her boiler patched in three places, her wheels worn to whispers. But she was the only engine left with steam in her, and the stationmaster had grabbed Tomas by the collar and said the word *refugees* like it was a curse.

The compartment door slid open behind him.

"You're the driver?"

Tomas turned. A woman stood in the doorway, a leather satchel pressed to her chest like a shield. She was small, dark-haired, with ink stains on her fingers that matched the smudge on her jaw. Her eyes were the thing though—quick, measuring, the eyes of someone used to counting exits.

"I'm the fireman," he said. "Driver's already up front."

"Close enough." She stepped into the cab, uninvited, and let the door slam behind her. Outside, the platform had become a crush of bodies. A soldier was trying to push a family back from the edge. He wasn't succeeding.

"You shouldn't be here," Tomas said. "Passenger cars are behind."

"Passenger cars are full." She said it without rancor, just fact. "I checked. So I walked forward until I found an unlocked door." Her fingers found a loose rivet on the bulkhead and worried at it. "I'm Esin. I need to get to the border."

Something in the way she said it. Tomas looked at her more closely. The satchel was worn, the leather soft from handling. A corner of paper peeked from the flap—newsprint, dense black type.

"You're the one they're looking for," he said.

She didn't deny it. "They're looking for a lot of people tonight."

The engine shuddered. A whistle blast from the front—the driver, old Mbano, signaling the last of the passengers aboard. Tomas checked the pressure gauge. The needle trembled at the red line. She'd hold, barely.

"Stay behind the coal bunker," he said. "If anyone comes asking, you're my assistant. You don't speak unless I say so."

Esin smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "I speak four languages. I'll manage."

The train lunged forward.

---

They cleared the station at speed, past the barriers and the soldiers who'd given up trying to hold the line, past the market stalls burning in the street, past a man standing on the roof of a truck with a megaphone shouting words Tomas couldn't hear over the engine's roar. The city fell away behind them like a discarded coat, its fires painting the sky in shades of amber and grief.

Tomas worked the coal. It was good work, honest work, the rhythm of shovel and sweat that had kept him sane through the long years of colonial rule and its slow, ugly dying. He'd been born on this line—literally, his mother used to say, in a carriage between Kano and the coast, mid-journey, mid-monsoon. He'd spent his life on it, learning every curve and gradient, every place the track sagged and every bridge that needed a prayer.

And now he was driving a train full of ghosts out of a burning city, with a journalist hiding behind his coal bunker.

"You want to tell me what's in the satchel?" he called over his shoulder.

Esin had wedged herself into the corner, the satchel clutched in her lap. In the glow of the firebox, her face was all angles and shadows. "Truth," she said.

"That's a heavy word."

"It's a heavy thing." She paused. "There's a story in here. Names. Documents. Proof that the withdrawal isn't a retreat—it's a handover. The British aren't leaving because they lost. They're leaving because they found someone worse to give it to."

Tomas shoveled coal. The heat rolled over him. "And you're going to print that."

"Someone has to." Her jaw set. "The newspapers are all shut down. The editors are either in jail or on this train. If this story doesn't get out, no one will ever know what happened here. No one will ever know who signed what, who sold whom."

The train hit a rough patch of track. The wheels clattered, the cab shook, and for a moment Tomas felt the old familiar lurch of the axle trying to leave the rail. He held his breath, felt the rhythm catch again, and exhaled.

"You understand what's waiting at the border?" he said. "The last report I heard, they've got tanks. Not British. Someone else's."

Esin was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was softer than he'd heard it. "I know."

"Then you know this might be a one-way trip."

"I know." She looked at him, and in the firelight her eyes were steady. "I've been on one-way trips before. This is the first one that matters."

---

They ran north through the night, through the flatlands that stretched like a held breath toward the mountains. The country was emptying. They passed villages that were already dark, farms where the livestock had been left to wander, fields gone to seed. Once, they passed a column of people walking south along the road that paralleled the tracks—men carrying bundles, women with children on their hips, an old man pushing a wheelbarrow with everything he owned. They didn't look up as the train thundered past.

Tomas thought about them. He thought about how the train was carrying maybe three hundred people, and how the road was carrying thousands, and how none of them were going to find what they were looking for.

Mbano called down from the front. "Water's low. We need to stop at the spur."

Tomas checked the gauge. Mbano was right—the tank was nearly dry, and the next station with a decent well was hours east. The spur was a siding near a village called Dir, a watering point used by the old freight runs. It had been abandoned for years, but the well was still there, if it hadn't been poisoned or blocked.

"Five minutes," Tomas called back. "No more."

The brakes groaned. The train slowed, and the night slid past the windows—dark, empty, watchful. They came to a stop beside the spur, and Tomas swung down from the cab with a bucket in each hand. The air was cold and smelled of dust and dry grass. Somewhere in the dark, a dog was barking.

Esin appeared at the cab door. "I'll come."

"Stay."

"I can carry buckets."

"You can carry that satchel." He gestured at the dark around them. "This isn't your country, is it?"

"It's everyone's country tonight."

Tomas didn't argue. He found the well at the edge of the spur, a stone ring half-swallowed by weeds. The rope was rotten but the bucket was intact, and he worked it down into the dark until he felt the splash, then hauled it up hand over hand. The water was cold and clean. He filled the buckets, carried them back, poured them into the tank. The engine hissed and steamed.

On the fourth trip, he heard it.

Not the dog. Something else. A sound carried on the wind—engines, distant but coming closer. Trucks, maybe, or armored cars. He straightened, listening, the bucket forgotten in his hand.

Esin was beside him suddenly, her voice low. "How far to the border?"

"Six hours, with luck."

"They won't give us six hours."

Tomas looked at her. In the starlight, her face was pale and determined. "No," he agreed. "They won't."

He dumped the last bucket, swung up into the cab, and pulled the whistle cord. The sound split the night, a long raw cry that echoed off the hills. Ahead, Mbano opened the throttle, and the wheels began to turn.

---

They made it through the night by inches.

Each mile was a negotiation with the track, the boiler, the water gauge, the dark. Tomas shoveled until his arms burned and his back screamed, and when he couldn't shovel he leaned against the coal bunker and watched the fires on the horizon—some behind them, some ahead. The border was a shifting thing, he knew. Lines drawn in ink, erased in blood. By the time they reached it, it might not exist at all.

"She's a good engine," he said, not to anyone in particular.

Esin was awake still, her eyes on the dark. "You talk to her?"

"She listens better than most people."

A pause. Then, softly: "What's her name?"

"Four-oh-seven. Never gave her a name. Didn't seem right, naming something you're going to scrap."

"Everything gets scrapped eventually." Esin's voice was distant. "But not everything gets a chance to run."

Tomas looked at her. The satchel was open on her lap, and in the glow of the firebox he saw the papers inside—yellowed, dense with type, the edges worn. A lifetime of secrets, pressed between leather.

"Will they read it?" he asked. "Wherever you're taking it?"

"I don't know." She closed the satchel, pressed the flap down. "But they'll have the chance. That's all anyone can ask for."

The train crested a rise, and for a moment the land opened up before them—a sweep of dark plains, and beyond them, the faintest blue line of mountains. The border. The end of the line.

Tomas checked the pressure gauge. The needle was dropping.

"We're not going to make it," he said. Not a question.

Esin looked at the mountains, then at him. "No," she said. "But we're going to come close."

And the train ran on, into the dark, with the fires behind them and the mountains ahead, and the truth in a leather satchel, waiting for its chance.
